Breaking the Symmetry

Transformation Symmetry in Different Magnetic Orders

a: Ferro

  • \(\mathbf{M} \neq 0\)
  • Breaks time-reversal symmetry \(\mathcal{T}\)
  • No spin degeneracy

b: Anti-Ferro

  • \(\mathbf{M} = 0\)
  • Preserves symmetry \(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}\)
  • Spin-degenerate electronic bands

c: Altermagnet

  • \(\mathbf{M} = 0\)
  • Breaks \(\mathcal{T}\) but preserves \(\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}\)
  • No spin degeneracy
Song, C., Bai, H., Zhou, Z. et al., Nat Rev Mater 10, 473-485 (2025)
The key quantity we will focus on is the optical conductivity tensor, which describes how the material responds to an applied electric field at different frequencies. In particular, we will see how the off-diagonal elements of this tensor can serve as a fingerprint for altermagnetism, distinguishing it from both ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic materials. If no magnetization is present, the conductivity tensor is diagonal. In a ferromagnet, the magnetization leads to off-diagonal elements due to the breaking of time-reversal symmetry. In an altermagnet, we also get off-diagonal elements, but they arise from the unique symmetry of the spin-splitting in k-space, even though the net magnetization is zero. This is a key point that we will explore in detail.

Optical Behavior

Mechanisms Generating Off-Diagonal Tensor Elements (\(\sigma_{xy}\))

Antisymmetric (\(\sigma_{xy} = -\sigma_{yx}\))

Requires Time-Reversal Breaking

  • External Magnetic Field (\(\mathbf{B} \neq 0\)): Explicitly breaks \(\mathcal{T}\) via the Lorentz force, cyclotron motion / Ordinary Hall effect.
  • Ferromagnetism (\(\mathbf{M} \neq 0\)): Spontaneous global \(\mathcal{T}\)-breaking coupled with Spin-Orbit Interaction
  • Altermagnetism (\(\mathbf{M} = 0\)): Spontaneous \(\mathcal{T}\)-breaking through alternating crystal symmetries.

Symmetric (\(\sigma_{xy} = \sigma_{yx}\))

Structural & Reciprocal Origins

  • Crystal Misalignment: Choosing a laboratory coordinate framework that is rotated relative to the principal crystallographic axes.
  • Linear Birefringence / Dichroism: Intrinsic optical anisotropy in lower-symmetry crystal systems (like the orthorhombic structure of \(FeSb_2\)).
  • Piezomagnetic / Magnetoelectric Effects: Strain-induced or voltage-induced modifications to the tensor framework
  • We can divide the off diag elements into symmetric and antisymmetric case
  • symmetric is related to some structural symmetry breaking
  • to identify an altermagnet we need to isolate the altermagnetic origin

Structural Origin of $\sigma_{xy}$: Low-Symmetry Monoclinic/Orthorhombic Systems

Example: Structural Monoclinic Angle Alignment

Internal crystal axes $(a, b, c)$ do not match an orthogonal laboratory coordinate framework $(x, y, z)$:

  • Purely Structural: Occurs in completely non-magnetic, time-reversal symmetric insulators.
  • The Consequence: Electric fields applied along the laboratory $x$-axis naturally induce a polarization current along the laboratory $y$-axis due to the tilted unit cell shape.
  • Tensor Property: Enforces a strictly symmetric off-diagonal element: $\sigma_{xy} = \sigma_{yx}$.

MOKKA: The 45-Degree Fast Protocol

Broad band measuring in extreme environments

Setup

  • Polarizer and Analyzer are fixed at 45° to each other
  • This geometry maximizes sensitivity to rotation

Analysis

  • Detector intensity proportional to the Kerr angle, for small angles: $$ I_{\pm45^\circ} \approx C \cdot R(\omega) \frac{1 \pm 2\theta_K(\omega)}{2} $$
  • Measure intensity ratio at positive and negative magnetic fields and isolate $\theta_K$: $$ \rho_{\pm45^\circ}(B) = \frac{I_{\pm45^\circ}(+B)}{I_{\pm45^\circ}(-B)} \approx 1 \pm 4\theta_K(B) $$
Levallois et al., Rev. Sci. Instrum. 86, 033906 (2015)
  • This leads to the 'Fast Protocol'. By fixing the polarizer and analyzer at 45 degrees, we eliminate all moving parts. The system is now at its most sensitive point to any small rotation.
  • the intensity becomes linear to kerr rotation
  • By flipping the magnetic field and taking an intensity ratio, all common factors cancel out, leaving a signal directly proportional to the Kerr angle.
  • common measurement technique, for example used in iron antimonide paper

  • Velocity Matrix Elements.

2. The Topology (Matrix Elements)

  • \(\langle \hat{v}_x \rangle \langle \hat{v}_y \rangle\) contains spin-sign
  • In altermagnets, this product survives the BZ sum

Essentially, the optical conductivity is a sum of all possible electronic transitions from an occupied band ($n$) to an unoccupied band ($m$). First, the denominator: when the photon energy $\hbar\omega$ matches the band energy difference, we see a resonance peak. In $FeSb_2$, this identified the $0.1 \text{ eV}$ transition. Second, the velocity matrix elements serve as selection rules. In standard AFMs with $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry, contributions at $k$ and $\mathcal{PT}k$ cancel out. However, altermagnetic spin-polarization allows for a net non-zero sum, creating a finite $\sigma_{xy}$ without net magnetization.

A Giant MOKE in a "Simple" Antiferromagnet

The Candidate: Hematite

  • $\alpha-\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ is a collinear antiferromagnetic insulator.
  • With zero net magnetization (M=0), it was long considered "invisible" to MOKE.
  • But crystal structure allows for the breaking of $\mathcal{T}t\mathcal{S}$ symmetry.

The Experimental Surprise

  • A giant spontaneous Kerr effect ($\theta_K \approx 80 \text{ mdeg}$) is observed.
  • The tiny net magnetization ($\Delta M \approx 0.006 \mu_B/\text{Fe}$) is far too small to explain this signal.
H. Yoshimochi et al., arXiv:2601.13723v1 [cond-mat.str-el] (2026)

The Answer is Symmetry

The Morin Transition as an "On/Off" Switch

Easy-Plane Phase (T > 260 K)

  • Spins lie in the basal plane.
  • The magnetic point group (2/m) allows for non-zero off-diagonal elements ($\sigma_{yz}$).
  • This symmetry creates a "fictitious magnetic field" generating the MOKE signal.

Easy-Axis Phase (T < 260 K)

  • Spins reorient along the c-axis during the "Morin transition".
  • The new magnetic point group (3'm) forbids any off-diagonal elements.
  • The spontaneous Kerr effect vanishes, proving it is tied to the specific AFM order.
H. Yoshimochi et al., arXiv:2601.13723v1 [cond-mat.str-el] (2026)

DFT Confirms the Microscopic Origin

Theory vs. Experiment for the Easy-Plane Phase

Decisive Test

  • First-principles calculations were performed for the easy-plane AFM state.
  • Crucially, the simulation was run assuming zero net magnetization (M=0).

Perfect Match

  • The simulation perfectly reproduces the experimental Kerr rotation ($\theta_K$) and ellipticity ($\eta_K$).
  • This includes the spectral shape, peak position (~2.5 eV), and absolute magnitude.
  • This provides conclusive evidence that the giant MOKE is an intrinsic property of the TtS-broken AFM order itself.
