Talking Points:

"We've discussed how altermagnetic symmetry can generate off-diagonal conductivity. Now let's look at a real-world example: α-Fe₂O₃, or common hematite."

"This is a classic material: a room-temperature, collinear antiferromagnet. With M=0, conventional wisdom said it should have no Kerr effect."

"However, its crystal structure has non-magnetic oxygen ions staggered between the magnetic iron ions. This structure is a candidate for breaking the TtS symmetry."

The Central Question: "Can a collinear AFM with M≈0 generate a Kerr effect, and if so, where does it come from?"


Talking Points:
"At room temperature, MOKE measurements reveal a stunning result. We see a large, spontaneous Kerr rotation of ~80 millidegrees—a value typical for a good ferromagnet."

"But when we look at the magnetization, it's almost zero. The tiny remnant moment is three orders of magnitude too small to explain this signal."

"The definitive proof is here: as we apply a field, the magnetization increases, but the Kerr angle does not change. This unambiguously proves that the Kerr effect is not driven by the net magnetization."


SLIDE 2

Talking Points:

"The origin lies in the magnetic symmetry. Above 260 K, in the 'easy-plane' phase, the magnetic point group allows for a non-zero off-diagonal conductivity, σ_yz."

"However, α-Fe₂O₃ has a trick up its sleeve. Below 260 K, it undergoes the Morin transition. The spins flop to the 'easy-axis' direction."

"This changes the symmetry, and the new magnetic point group forbids an off-diagonal response."

"As you can see in the data, the spontaneous Kerr signal vanishes precisely at this transition. This is a perfect demonstration that the MOKE is a direct consequence of the specific antiferromagnetic order."


SLIDE 3

Talking Points:

"To confirm this picture, we turn to DFT simulations, using the Kubo formula we discussed earlier."

"The crucial step: the calculation was performed for the easy-plane antiferromagnetic state assuming zero net magnetization."

"The result is a near-perfect match. The simulation quantitatively reproduces the spectral shape, the peak position (~2.5 eV), and the absolute magnitude of the Kerr rotation and ellipticity."

"This provides the final, conclusive evidence: the giant magneto-optical Kerr effect in α-Fe₂O₃ is an intrinsic property of its TtS-broken collinear antiferromagnetic order."
