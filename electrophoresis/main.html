Free-Solution Electrophoresis

Electrophoretic mobility is defined as the steady-state drift velocity per unit electric field:$$\mu = \frac{v_{\text{drift}}}{E}$$ We have 3 driving effects:

  • Direct electrostatic driving force: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{el}} = Q_{\text{eff}}\mathbf{E}$
  • Hydrodynamic drag from the solvent
  • Electro-osmotic retardation: Drag from the oppositely charged counterions against solvent molecules

To simulate DNA electrophoresis without tracking every solvent atom, we adopt the standard Kremer-Grest coarse-grained bead-spring model.
Here, a DNA segment is represented as $N$ charged spherical beads. Each bead represents approximately one to two base pairs and carries a bare charge of $-1e$.
Excluded volume is enforced between all beads and explicit counterions using the Weeks-Chandler-Andersen (WCA) potential. By truncating the Lennard-Jones potential at its minimum of $2^{1/6}\sigma$ and shifting it, we obtain a purely repulsive, smooth force that prevents particle overlap without adding artificial van der Waals attraction.
To maintain the physical connectivity of our DNA backbone, we use FENE bonds rather than standard harmonic springs. The logarithmic potential wall at $\Delta r_{\max}$ ensures that even under strong external electric driving or hydrodynamic shear, the bond cannot stretch beyond a hard limit, preventing the chain from passing through itself. In our simulations, we vary $N$ from 2 up to 30 monomers to systematically probe how this connectivity influences both diffusion and electrophoretic mobility.

Non-Bonded Potential: Weeks-Chandler-Andersen (WCA)

$$V_{\text{WCA}}(r) = \begin{cases} 4\epsilon \left[ \left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\dfrac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6} \right] + \epsilon, & r \le r_c = 2^{1/6}\sigma \\ 0, & r > r_c \end{cases}$$

Bonded Potential: Finitely Extensible Nonlinear Elastic (FENE)

$$V_{\text{FENE}}(r) = -\frac{1}{2} k \Delta r_{\max}^2 \ln \left[ 1 - \left( \frac{r - r_0}{\Delta r_{\max}} \right)^2 \right]$$

Counter Ions & Solvent Coupling

Explicit Counterions

Explicit models resolve each counterion as an individual particle (a charged WCA sphere) that moves dynamically (Newton equation of motion).

Solvent Coupling

  • We exceed the Manning threshold $\xi = \ell_B / b > 1$, with $\ell_B$ with $b$ the charge spacing
  • Some counterions condenses directly onto the polymer backbone.
  • Implicit screening would not cover this effect.

Solvent Coupling

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{fluid}} = -\mathbf{F}_{\text{drag}, i} = \zeta \left( \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}_i) \right)$$

Counterions generate a localized fluid counter-flow (electro-osmotic flow) that rushes past the polymer in opposite direction. This produces hydrodynamic shielding and electro-osmotic retardation, fundamental for short-chain mobility peak ($\mu(N)$ anomaly) and length-independent plateau ($\mu \sim N^0$) in long chains.

Free-Draining vs. Non-Free-Draining Polymers

Question from the Worksheet

Maintaining a constant monomer concentration

  • Lattice-Boltzmann (LB) method as hydrodynamic solver used to couple the particles to the fluid
  • To properly compare different chain lengths ($N = 2 \ldots 30$), the monomer concentration must be kept constant. This is done by adjusting the simulation box volume as $N$ changes, ensuring the Debye screening length and counterion density remain consistent across all simulations.

"What is the difference between a free-draining and non-free draining polymer?"

  • Free-Draining (Rouse Model): The polymer does not perturb the solvent. The solvent flows freely through the polymer coil. Hydrodynamic interactions are ignored. Diffusion scales as $D \propto N^{-1}$
  • Non-Free-Draining (Zimm Model): The polymer drags the solvent along with it. Hydrodynamic interactions (HI) between monomers are crucial. The coil acts like a solid sphere of radius, Diffusion scales as $D \propto R_{H}^{-1} \propto N^{-\nu}$

Mobility Behavior at different Chain Length

Long Range

Electrostatic driving Force: $$F_{\text{el}} = N q_{\text{eff}} E$$ Hydrodynamic Drag: $$F_{\text{drag}} = \Gamma · v_{\text{drift}} = (N · \gamma) · v_{\text{drift}}$$
Combined: $$N q_{\text{eff}} E = N \gamma v_{\text{drift}}$$$$ \implies \mu = \frac{v_{\text{drift}}}{E} = \frac{q_{\text{eff}}}{\gamma} \propto N^0$$ Because $\mu$ is invariant with $N$, standard free-solution electrophoresis cannot separate long DNA fragments by size.

Short Range

  • Very Short Chains ($N < 6$)
    • Counterion cloud incomplete
    • Weak internal shielding
    • Drag rises faster than charge
  • Peak Region ($N \approx 6-10$)
    • Optimum hydrodynamic shielding
    • Counterion cloud drag is fully active
  • Long Chains ($N > 20$)
    • Full counterion cloud
    • electro-osmotic drag cancels size dependence

The Hockney-Eastwood Optimization

Find a function that minimized the RMS Error:

$$Q = \frac{1}{V} \int_V \left\vert{} \mathbf{F}_{\text{mesh}}(\mathbf{r}) - \mathbf{F}_{\text{exact}}(\mathbf{r}) \right\vert{}^2 d^3\mathbf{r} \longrightarrow \text{Minimum}$$

To measure electrophoretic mobility in our simulations, we have two fundamental approaches grounded in statistical physics: an equilibrium zero-field approach based on Green-Kubo relations, and a direct, non-equilibrium explicit-field approach. The zero-field method observes the spontaneous thermal fluctuations of the DNA and ions at complete equilibrium and uses the fluctuation-dissipation theorem to compute mobility. In contrast, the explicit-field method mimics a laboratory experiment: we apply a small electric field $\mathbf{E}$, drive the polymer, and measure its steady-state drift velocity. Over the next slides, we will look at the mathematical formulations of both.

Equilibrium vs. Non-Equilibrium

Measuring Electrophoretic Mobility

Zero-Field (Green-Kubo)

  • Field $E = 0$ (Equilibrium)
  • Measures spontaneous current fluctuation
  • Pure linear response regime

Explicit-Field (Driven MD)

  • Field $E > 0$ (Driven state)
  • Measures direct drift velocity$v_{\text{drift}}$
  • Requires weak-field check
In the zero-field approach, the simulation runs at strictly $\mathbf{E} = 0$. By the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, we obtain both self-diffusion $D$ and electrophoretic mobility $\mu$ from time-correlation functions. The diffusion coefficient $D$ is the time integral of the center-of-mass velocity autocorrelation function. The mobility is given by the cross-correlation between the center-of-mass velocity of the DNA chain and the total electrical current of the entire system $\mathbf{j}_{\text{tot}}$, which includes both the polymer beads and the migrating counterions. Because this evaluates cross-correlations without an external driving force, long integration times—typically on the order of $10^7$ timesteps—are required to resolve the correlation decay before statistical noise dominates the integral tail.

Equilibrium

Zero-Field Approach: Green-Kubo

Diffusion Coefficient $D$

$$D = \frac{1}{3} \int_{0}^{\infty} \left\langle \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}(0) \cdot \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}(t) \right\rangle \, dt$$

Electrophoretic mobility $\mu$

$$\mu_{\text{GK}} = \frac{1}{3 k_B T} \int_{0}^{\infty} \left\langle \mathbf{v}_{\text{CM}}(t) \cdot \mathbf{j}_{\text{tot}}(0) \right\rangle \, dt$$

with $\mathbf{j}_{\text{tot}}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{all}}} q_i \, \mathbf{v}_i(t)$.

  • A system's linear response to a weak external perturbation is entirely encoded in its spontaneous equilibrium thermal fluctuations.
  • High statistical noise in the integral tail at long times $t$
    requires running long trajectories ($10^7$ steps) and truncating or fitting the tail.
In the explicit-field method, we apply a small, constant electric field along a Cartesian axis. Each charged entity—monomer and counterion alike—feels an explicit external force $q_i \mathbf{E}$. After discarding initial equilibration steps, the polymer reaches a steady-state terminal drift velocity $\langle v_{\text{CM}, x} \rangle$ where electrostatic pulling balances total hydrodynamic and electro-osmotic drag.The mobility is simply this drift velocity divided by the applied field strength. The primary experimental check here is ensuring we remain strictly in the linear regime—for instance, using $E = 0.1$. If the field is too strong, it will artificially stretch the polymer or distort the counterion cloud beyond linear response theory

Non-Equilibrium

Explicit-Field Method / Driven MD

Electrophoretic mobility $\mu$

$$\mu = \frac{\langle v_{\text{CM}, x} \rangle}{E_x}$$

Measure the steady-state center-of-mass velocity component $\langle v_{\text{CM}, x} \rangle$ after equilibration

  • Apply a constant, homogeneous external electric field.
  • Electric field too weak: Signal drowned out by thermal Brownian noise.
  • Electric field too strong: Non-linear conformational stretching, artificial heating, or field-induced counterion strip-off.
To summarize how these methods compare and when each should be chosen:The Green-Kubo zero-field method is unmatched when you need an unperturbed, multi-property baseline from a single trajectory. In one equilibrium run, you can extract the radius of gyration, conformational relaxation times, diffusion coefficients, and mobility without risking field-induced distortions. Conversely, the explicit-field method is the workhorse for high-throughput parameter sweeps—such as varying the chain length $N$ from 2 to 30. Its superior signal-to-noise ratio provides faster statistical convergence. Furthermore, it is the only method that allows us to explore non-linear phenomena, such as field-induced chain uncoiling at very high electric fields.

Method Comparison: When is Each Method Appropriate?

Zero-Field Green-Kubo

  • $\mathbf{E} = 0$
  • Low SNR, slow convergence
  • Simultaneously measuring equilibrium conformational dynamics, radius of gyration $R_g$, diffusion coefficients $D$, and mobility $\mu$ from a single trajectory without perturbing the phase-space distribution.
  • Avoiding non-equilibrium artifacts, artificial alignment, or unphysical polarization of the counterion cloud.

Explicit-Field Non-Equilibrium

  • $\mathbf{E} > 0$
  • High SNR, fast convergence
  • Conducting production measurements of mobility $\mu(N)$ across wide parameter sweeps due to rapid convergence and superior signal-to-noise ratio.
  • Studying non-linear electrophoretic phenomena, field-induced chain stretching, and high-field structural transitions beyond linear response limits.

Parameter comparison

Scaling of the Diffusion Coefficient $D(N)$

  • The Data: $D$ drops by nearly an order of magnitude from $N=2$ ($D \approx 0.020$) to $N=30$ ($D \approx 0.0035$).
  • Rouse vs. Zimm Scaling Context:
  • In a free-draining Rouse polymer (no hydrodynamic interactions): $D \propto N^{-1}$.
  • In a non-free-draining Zimm polymer (with hydrodynamic interactions): $D \propto R_H^{-1} \propto N^{-\nu} \approx N^{-0.588}$ to $N^{-0.6}$.
  • The Ratio Check:$\frac{D(N=2)}{D(N=30)} = \frac{0.0202}{0.00354} \approx 5.71$
  • Pure Rouse prediction: $30 / 2 = 15$.
  • Pure Zimm prediction ($N^{-0.6}$): $(30/2)^{0.6} \approx 15^{0.6} \approx 5.08$.
  • Takeaway: The scaling ratio ($\approx 5.71$) is close to the Zimm prediction ($5.08$) and far from the Rouse limit ($15$). This quantitatively proves that the Lattice-Boltzmann fluid is actively mediating hydrodynamic interactions across the chain.
Systematic Offset: Zero-Crossing vs. Exponential Tail Fit
  • The Data: $D_{\text{Zero-Cross}}$ is systematically $8\%\text{-}40\%$ higher than $D_{\text{Exp-Fit}}$ across all lengths (e.g., $0.00354$ vs. $0.00250$ for $N=30$).
  • The Methodological Cause:
  • The Zero-Crossing method integrates all numerical data points up to where the curve first drops below zero. Any weak, positive statistical noise or long-time hydrodynamic algebraic tail ($t^{-3/2}$) before the zero point adds directly to the total integral.
  • The Exponential Fit method assumes a pure single-exponential decay $A e^{-t/\tau}$. While it eliminates random long-time noise, it tends to slightly underestimate the slow algebraic hydrodynamic tail of the VACF, acting as a lower-bound estimate of the true diffusion coefficient.
